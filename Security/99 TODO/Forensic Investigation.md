# Forensic Investigation

## Introduction

Forensic investigation is a branch of forensic science that encompasses the recovery, investigation, examination, and analysis of material found on electronic devices. It is a rapidly growing field, expanding at the same rate as cyberattacks and virtual crimes. Electronic evidence is a component of nearly all criminal activities, making digital forensic support crucial for police investigations.
## Summary

### What is Digital Forensics?

Digital forensics is a branch of cybersecurity focused on the recovery and investigation of material found on digital devices and related to cybercrimes. Originally used as a synonym for "computer forensics," the term has expanded to cover the investigation of all devices that store digital data.

Digital forensic specialists also work in the private sector as part of cybersecurity and information security teams to identify the causes of data breaches, leaks, cyberattacks, and other threats. Digital forensics can also be part of incident response to help recover or identify sensitive data or **Personally Identifiable Information (PII)** lost or stolen during a crime.

Essentially, it is the process of discovering and interpreting electronic data. The goal is to preserve evidence in its most original form while conducting a structured investigation—collecting, identifying, and validating digital information to reconstruct past events. This context is most often for data usage in a court of law, though it can be applied in other instances.

### Types of Digital Forensics

Digital evidence falls under the same legal guidelines as other forms of evidence. Generally, laws dealing with digital evidence relate to:

- **Integrity**: Ensuring that the act of seizing and acquiring digital media does not modify the evidence (whether the original or the copy).
    
- **Authenticity**: The ability to confirm the information's integrity. The **chain of custody** from the crime scene to analysis and finally to the courtroom—in the form of an audit trail—is vital for establishing authenticity.
    

Popular types of forensic investigations include:

- **Computer Forensics**: Concerned with evidence found on computers and digital storage media.
    
- **Network Forensics**: Involves analyzing network events, intrusions, and transmitted data packets to detect attacks.
    
- **Forensic Data Analysis**: Related to the study and examination of databases and their associated metadata to uncover fraudulent patterns.
    
- **Mobile Device Forensics**: Focused on recovering digital evidence from mobile devices (phones, PDAs, GPS, tablets) using sound forensic methods.
    
- **Malware Forensics**: Deals with identifying malicious code to study its payload, such as viruses and worms.
    

Other types include the analysis of email, disks, memory, and cloud services.

### Digital Forensic Techniques

Digital forensics involves creating copies of a compromised device and using various tools to examine information. These techniques help inspect **unallocated disk space** and hidden folders for encrypted, damaged, or deleted files.

Common techniques include:

- **Reverse Steganography**: Cybercriminals use steganography to hide data within files or messages. Reverse steganography analyzes the data hash of a specific file to find hidden information that might alter the underlying sequence.
    
- **Stochastic Forensics**: Helps analyze and reconstruct digital activity that does not generate "digital artifacts" (unintentional data changes).
    
- **Cross-drive Analysis**: Also known as anomaly detection, this finds similarities to provide context, allowing an investigator to correlate information across multiple data sources.
    
- **Live Analysis**: Performed on the operating system while the device is running, using tools to extract volatile data stored in **RAM** or cache.
    
- **Deleted File Recovery**: Also known as **data carving**, this involves searching a system for file fragments that were partially deleted but left traces elsewhere.
    

### Phases of Digital Forensics

Various methodologies define how examiners should collect, process, and extract data.

1. **Identification**: Determining what evidence is present, where it is stored, and in what format.
    
2. **Preservation**: Isolating and protecting data to prevent tampering.
    
3. **Collection**: Acquiring digital evidence, usually by seizing physical assets like hard drives or phones.
    
4. **Examination**: A precursor to analysis involving a careful inspection for secondary details.
    
5. **Analysis**: The crucial step of piecing together fragments and recovering deleted files to prove or refute a case.
    
6. **Interpretation**: Drawing conclusions after reconstructing the crime scene.
    
7. **Documentation**: Preparing a detailed report on the entire investigation.
    
8. **Presentation**: Summarizing and explaining conclusions in simple, natural language for a hearing or court.
    

#### Pros and Cons

|**Advantages**|**Disadvantages**|
|---|---|
|Ensures computer system integrity.|Evidence must be proven tamper-free to be admissible.|
|Produces evidence for court.|Producing and storing electronic records is very expensive.|
|Helps companies recover info after a breach.|Legal professionals need extensive IT knowledge.|
|Efficiently tracks global cybercriminals.|Lack of technical knowledge can lead to poor results.|
