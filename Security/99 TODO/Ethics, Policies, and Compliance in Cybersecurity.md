### Ethics and Cybersecurity

Ethics can be understood as the code by which one must live, work, and treat others. Humanity has always searched for guiding principles to lead its actions and shape its communities.

There are certain overarching ethical principles that shape our understanding of the world around us. They guide us through our private lives and often shape the way we carry out our professional work.

When we think of cybersecurity professionals, we sometimes overlook the humanitarian aspect of their profession. However, on the other side of the screen, there is an entire team of professionals striving to protect our personal data, combat malicious attacks, and manage a wide range of security risks.

Ethical issues arising in the cybersecurity domain vary based on the activities implemented by stakeholders and legal requirements regarding the level of security. The intensity of risks that may arise for individuals must also be considered.

With the increasing advent of new technologies, ethical risks related to cybersecurity can occur in any area of daily life—economy, health, public safety, transportation, etc.—and cause different levels of damage to individuals.

Cybersecurity professionals must behave ethically and prove to their supervisors through their actions that they are worthy of overseeing valuable information. According to SecurityIntelligence, there are no mandatory standards for cyber ethics issues that professionals are required to follow, but this does not prevent organizations from creating their own rules and compliance measures.

### Policies and Compliance

A cybersecurity policy is a written document containing behavioral and technical guidelines for all employees to ensure maximum protection against cybersecurity incidents. The policy contains information about a company's or organization's policies, procedures, technological safeguards, and operational countermeasures in the event of a cybersecurity incident.

Cybersecurity policies are important because cyberattacks and data breaches are potentially expensive. At the same time, employees are often the weak links in an organization's security. Employees share passwords, click on malicious URLs and attachments, use unapproved cloud applications, and neglect to encrypt sensitive files.

Cybersecurity procedures explain the rules for how employees, consultants, partners, board members, and other end users access online applications and internet resources, send data over networks, and otherwise practice responsible security.

The IT department, usually the CIO or CISO, is primarily responsible for all information security policies. However, other stakeholders often contribute to the policy depending on their expertise and roles within the organization.

When developing an organization's cybersecurity policy, do not forget the following topics:

- Organization-wide password requirements.
    
- Email security measures.
    
- Access control measures and access permissions.
    
- Descriptions of how to handle sensitive data.
    
- Implementation of a set of standards for social media and internet access.
    
- A plan on how to prepare for a cyber incident.
    
- Security controls for updates and remote access.
    
- Seeking guidance from norms, standards, and laws such as the LGPD.
    

Having a clear set of rules and guidelines for each of these will help build a successful cybersecurity policy that is easy to understand and essential for maintaining a positive cyber posture.

#### Detailed Examples:

- **Acceptable Use Policy (AUP):** An AUP is used to specify the restrictions and practices that an employee using organizational IT assets must agree to in order to access the corporate network or systems.
    
- **Data Breach Response Policy:** The goal of the data breach response policy is to describe the process for handling an incident and remedying the impact on business operations and customers.
    
- **Access Control Policy (ACP):** An ACP defines standards for user access, network access controls, and system software controls.
    
- **Remote Access Policy:** According to an IBM study, remote work during COVID-19 increased data breach costs; a remote access directive describes and defines procedures for remotely accessing the organization's internal networks.
    
- **Update Policy:** The update policy contains information determining when and how computers and other devices can download security updates, fixes, or new features.
    

#### To Start a Compliance Program, Consider These Factors:

1. **Identify the type of data you work with and applicable requirements:** To start working toward compliance, first discover which regulations or laws you need to comply with.
    
2. **Appoint a CISO:** Most companies are too small to justify hiring a dedicated CISO; however, any employee with the specialized knowledge and work ethic can be appointed to manage cybersecurity, whether it be an IT director or IT manager.
    
3. **Perform risk and vulnerability assessments:** Almost all cybersecurity compliance requirements demand risk and vulnerability assessments. They are essential for determining the organization's most critical security flaws and identifying existing controls.
    
4. **Implement policies, procedures, and process controls:** Cybersecurity is not just about technology. Having policies and procedures in place to mitigate risks is also crucial for compliance and security. No technical safeguard can stop an employee determined to download malware or visit risky websites. Examples of non-technical controls include:
    
    - Mandatory cybersecurity training for employees.
        
    - Fully documented policies and procedures.
        
    - Audit and accountability procedures.
        
    - Appointing a CISO.
        
    - Performing risk and vulnerability assessments.
        
5. **Review and Testing:** Review the applicable requirements you need to meet and ensure you regularly test your controls. As businesses change and expand, it can be easy to let cybersecurity slip, but regular testing ensures you remain in compliance. It is advisable to continuously evaluate compliance as new requirements appear and existing ones change.
    

In many cases, companies are required to comply with all relevant government laws, rules, and regulations, including those regarding data privacy. There is no choice here: either the organization is in compliance or it risks losing its permission to operate. This is a worldwide trend, and in Brazil, the **General Data Protection Law (LGPD)** establishes rules and fines for companies that fail to comply with its regulations.

### What is the CSIRT?

**CSIRT: Computer Security Incident Response Team** – is a group of IT professionals that provides an organization with services and support related to the assessment, management, and prevention of cybersecurity emergencies, as well as the coordination of incident response efforts.

The main objective of a CSIRT is to respond to technological security incidents quickly and efficiently, thereby regaining control and minimizing damage. This involves following the four phases of incident response from the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST):

- Preparation.
    
- Detection and analysis.
    
- Containment, eradication, and recovery.
    
- Post-incident activity.
    

How a CSIRT is structured depends on the organization's needs, such as the requirement for 24/7 coverage, employee availability, full-time vs. part-time members, and operational costs.

#### Common CSIRT Structures Include:

- **Centralized CSIRT:** A single incident response team serves the entire organization, and all response resources are contained within a dedicated unit.
    
- **Distributed CSIRT:** Multiple independent incident response teams exist, often depending on the organization's broad geographical scope or the location of major facilities.
    
- **Hybrid CSIRT:** Combines centralized and distributed attributes. The central component usually works full-time, while the distributed component consists of specialists who participate only when necessary during security events.
    
- **Outsourced CSIRT:** A useful option for companies lacking the resources for an internal team. This involves hiring contractors instead of employees or outsourcing specific tasks like digital forensics that are only needed occasionally.
    

An effective CSIRT requires members with various skills. Organizations must train employees to meet specific incident response needs. A baseline of technical skills—including security principles, vulnerabilities, programming, and network protocols—is required for daily tasks.
