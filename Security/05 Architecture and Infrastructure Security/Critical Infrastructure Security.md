# Critical Infrastructures

Critical Infrastructures (CI) comprise the facilities, services, assets, and property that possess strategic and vital dimensions for the socioeconomic functioning, safety, and national sovereignty of a State. Due to the systemic impact caused by failures in these sectors, cyber and physical security plans unanimously prioritize the pillar of **availability**.

The main sectors that integrate critical infrastructures are:

* **Telecommunications:** Data transmission networks, voice, internet links, and core traffic datacenters.
* **Energy:** Hydroelectric, thermoelectric, and nuclear power plants.
* **Transport and Logistics:** Distribution networks and transport of passengers and inputs.
* **Oil and Gas:** Extraction, refining, and fuel distribution chains.
* **Water and Sanitation:** Treatment stations and water distribution networks.
* **Supply and Food:** Food distribution centers and logistics supply chains.
* **Public Health:** Hospitals, emergency care networks, and pharmaceutical industries.
* **Public Safety:** Defense forces and emergency response systems.
* **Economic:** Banks, ATM networks, and stock exchanges.
* **Dams and Monuments:** Tailings containment or energy generation structures and national heritage sites.

## Governance and Regulatory Frameworks

Global governance over the protection of critical infrastructures intensified following the terrorist attacks of September 11, 2001, in the United States. The US government expanded protection directives in 2013, integrating agencies such as the FBI, CIA, NSA, and the Department of Homeland Security (DHS) in risk identification and technological support. The National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) developed a cybersecurity framework specific to these structures, and in 2018, the regulatory agency CISA (Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency) was established.

In Brazil, regulation evolved with the promulgation of Decree No. 9,573 of 2018, which instituted the National Critical Infrastructure Security Policy focused on risk analysis. In 2020, Decree No. 10,569 established the National Critical Infrastructure Security Strategy, developed by the Institutional Security Office (GSI) aligned with international CISA models. The Brazilian model provides direct State support for public and state-owned enterprises, leaving private institutions to invest their own resources and voluntarily adopt the NIST or GSI frameworks to mitigate risks of service discontinuity.

## Interdependencies and Vulnerabilities

Critical infrastructures exhibit high resource interdependence: nuclear power plants require external power grids to operate monitors and constant water flows to cool reactors; similarly, datacenters require electrical power and synchronous telecommunications links. The mitigation of cascading failures is achieved through asset redundancy and operational resilience, allowing the infrastructure to continue operating under constraints in crisis situations.

Technological convergence has connected the control of these industrial and distribution plants to data networks, making them targets for cyber threats. The case of the **Stuxnet** virus, discovered in 2010, exemplifies the sophistication of cyber warfare targeted against critical infrastructures. It was a malware developed in a tactical cooperation between the United States and Israel with the objective of paralyzing Iran's uranium enrichment program at the Natanz underground facility.

The plant operated under an *air gap* regime, physically isolated from external internet connections. The infection vector occurred physically, through a flash memory device (USB drive) introduced locally into the plant's internal networks. The malicious code utilized four simultaneous zero-day vulnerabilities and was specifically designed to intercept Siemens programmable logic controllers (PLCs) that managed the rotation of the uranium centrifuges.

Stuxnet altered the rotation frequency of the motors to destructive levels, causing irreversible physical damage to the centrifuge rotors. Simultaneously, the malware executed a log falsification technique, replicating stability and normal parameters on the control engineers' computers, which delayed the detection of the attack and the containment of the industrial sabotage.
